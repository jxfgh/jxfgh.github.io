A question has been dominating conversations about the future of work:

**Will AI replace software developers?**

It is a reasonable question. AI can now generate code, debug applications, build prototypes and automate many tasks that once required experienced engineers.

But there is an interesting contradiction in a [recent analysis of US career projections through 2035](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ael24TrS0ws).

Two of the most prominent high-paying careers on the list are:

- **No. 2: Software Developers** — approximately US$135,980
- **No. 1: Computer and Information Systems Managers** — approximately US$175,000

The source analysis projects continued employment growth for both occupations.

Why would software development remain attractive when AI can write code? And why might technology management become even more valuable when AI can also help with planning, analysis and reporting?

I think the answer lies in a shift from producing technical output to making decisions about what should be built, why it matters, and who is accountable for the result.

## 1. A surprising story from Anthropic

In an [interview on the Silicon Valley Girl YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D-SVFneJ_Nk&t=1818s), Felix Rieseberg, an engineering leader at Anthropic, described how his own work had changed.

He used to inspect application crashes, collect the relevant information and pass each issue to Claude.

He eventually realised that he was becoming a conveyor belt for the AI.

Instead of manually delivering one issue after another, he wanted Claude to have access to the relevant crash data, identify what needed fixing and work through the problem.

His role moved towards prototyping, exploring alternatives and evaluating which ideas were worth pursuing.

He also described spending more time thinking about users, empathy and how people actually work.

This is an important change.

The engineer did not become less valuable because AI could write code.

The nature of his contribution changed.

## 2. Software development: cheaper execution can create more demand

Consider a business that wants to build an internal application.

A few years ago, the process might have required a budget, a development team, a project plan and several months of work.

The business might decide the project is not worth the cost.

Now, AI-assisted development has lowered the cost of building a prototype or a small internal tool.

In one example discussed in the source video, a content production team used an AI development platform to build an internal tool connecting Notion, HubSpot and Gmail.

The tool consolidated information and produced daily follow-up tasks. A team member without an engineering background was involved in building it.

The practical lesson is not that professional engineers are no longer needed.

It is that lower development costs make more software projects economically viable.

Businesses can now consider building tools for smaller, more specialised problems that were previously ignored.

This can expand the amount of software businesses want to create.

However, generating code is only one part of delivering a reliable product.

Software still has to work with existing systems, protect data, handle failures, scale with demand and solve a real user problem.

The value of an engineer therefore moves beyond writing code.

It increasingly includes:

- Understanding the problem before building.
- Designing systems and integrations.
- Evaluating AI-generated code and architecture.
- Managing security, reliability and performance.
- Turning prototypes into products that people actually use.

There is also a difficult implication for early-career professionals.

When AI automates routine coding tasks, the traditional entry-level learning path may become narrower. The profession can grow while the expectations placed on new engineers rise.

The opportunity remains, but the route into the profession is changing.

## 3. Technology management: the value of deciding what not to build

The first-ranked role is even more interesting: Computer and Information Systems Managers.

Think about a bank planning an AI transformation.

- Customer service wants an AI assistant.
- Risk management wants better fraud detection.
- Finance wants automated reporting.
- IT wants to modernise its infrastructure.

Every department has a business case. Every vendor has a product. Every solution promises efficiency.

But someone has to decide:

- Which projects deserve investment?
- Which systems should be integrated, replaced or retired?
- How should data and security risks be managed?
- What happens when an AI system makes a wrong decision?
- And who is accountable?

This is not simply an IT administration problem.

It is a business leadership problem.

Technology managers increasingly need to understand the connection between architecture, operations, regulation, risk, budgets and customer outcomes.

The source video makes a particularly useful observation about why these roles remain valuable:

> The scarce capability is judgment under uncertainty and accountability for the outcome.

AI can generate technical options.

It cannot remove the organisation's need to choose between them and take responsibility for the consequences.

A technology leader is not valuable simply because they know which tools are available.

They are valuable because they understand which tools should be used, where the risks lie and what the business is trying to achieve.

## 4. A useful way to rethink your own job: Define, Execute, Evaluate

Economist Eric Brynjolfsson offers a useful framework for analysing work:

**Define. Execute. Evaluate.**

Almost every project includes three questions:

1. What problem are we trying to solve?
2. How do we execute the work?
3. Did the result achieve what we wanted?

AI is becoming increasingly effective at the execution stage.

Take financial crime detection as an example.

AI can analyse transaction data, identify suspicious patterns and generate investigation summaries.

But people still need to define what suspicious behaviour means in a particular context.

They must decide how to balance false positives and false negatives, assess customer impact, validate model performance and determine whether the system meets regulatory expectations.

Most importantly, someone must own the decision when the system gets it wrong.

The same logic applies to software development, healthcare, cybersecurity and business management.

The tasks change, but the need to define problems and evaluate outcomes remains.

## 5. What should professionals do now?

I would not advise everyone to move into software engineering or technology management.

Nor would I assume that a growing occupation is automatically a safe career choice.

Instead, I would start with a simple exercise.

Review the work you have done over the past two weeks.

Separate it into three categories:

- **Execution:** Repetitive tasks that AI may be able to perform.
- **Definition:** Work that requires understanding the business problem and deciding what matters.
- **Evaluation:** Work that requires professional judgment, independent verification and accountability.

Choose one repetitive task to automate.

Then use the time saved to take ownership of a more valuable problem.

For example, do not spend all your time reporting how many customers have left. Investigate why they are leaving and recommend what the organisation should do next.

That is a very different contribution.

## Final thought

The future of work is not simply a competition between humans and machines.

It is a question of how the value of work gets redistributed as execution becomes cheaper.

Software developers remain important because businesses still need people who can turn problems into reliable products.

Technology managers remain important because organisations need people who can make difficult decisions about technology, investment and risk.

The same principle applies well beyond technology.

AI can help us execute. Our advantage comes from knowing what is worth executing, how to judge the result, and when to take responsibility.

**When execution becomes cheaper, judgment becomes more valuable.**
